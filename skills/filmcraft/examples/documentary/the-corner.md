# The corner of the picture

A five-minute archival video essay for someone who recognizes Dorothea Lange’s *Migrant Mother* but has never examined its catalogue record. The viewer gets to make the discovery before the narrator names it. This is a complete recording draft with an estimated edit, ready for a table read. Timing includes comparison holds. The archive photographs are linked and credited here; retrieve the specific reproductions and check each rights advisory when producing a cut.

## Recording script

**1 • Look once**

Take a look at this photograph. Give it a few seconds. Where did your eye go first?

Mine goes to the woman’s hand, the one against her face. Then I notice the children beside her. It takes me longer to find the detail we came for. It’s down here, at the bottom right. Remember that corner. We’re going to look at the photograph again.

Here’s a second version. Same face, same hand, same children. Something at the edge has changed. Can you spot it before the pictures switch back?

**2 • Compare two copies**

Take the first corner on its own. A thumb curls around a pole. Now look at the same patch in the second picture. The pole is still there. The thumb is gone.

With two files on a screen, that could be a trick of the framing. So let’s give the two corners the same amount of room. Line up the pole. Look at the area on either side of it. Switch between the pictures again. The difference stays put.

You could keep guessing what happened at that edge. Or you could ask what, exactly, was scanned to make each of these files.

**3 • Follow the record**

The Library of Congress keeps a record for this photograph. Find the notes and read the line about the negative. The digital file was made from Lange’s original nitrate negative. In the nineteen thirties, that negative was retouched to remove the thumb.

There’s another object in the record. A print was made before the retouching. That print is where we can still see the thumb. One digital image takes us back to a piece of film that had been altered. The other takes us back to a print made earlier. The word “original” only gets us halfway there. Original *what*?

Imagine you’re choosing the image for a book. If you want to show the retouched photograph, you can name the negative. If you want to show what changed, you need the earlier print as well. A credit can help the next reader find the very detail your page is asking them to see.

**4 • Step back from the corner**

Now open the Library’s guide to the series. The familiar portrait has company. Look down the page. Each entry carries a description and a reproduction number. One entry points to the negative. Another identifies the digital file made from the earlier print. The list also leads to other photographs of the family.

There’s a note before the pictures. Two views of the mother and children, taken from an angle at a medium distance, apparently never reached this collection. The guide points elsewhere for them, to the Dorothea Lange Archive in Oakland.

That small word “apparently” does some work. It tells us how far the Library’s account goes. Even a page with this much detail has an edge. We can follow the lead without filling the gap with a scene of our own.

**5 • Look again**

Back to the whole photograph. You know where the change is now. Try looking without the outline around it. You can still find the face. You can still find the hand. And this time you can tell someone which version they’re looking at, and how to check.

There’s a question we haven’t answered. Why remove the thumb? These records establish that it was removed. They don’t give us a reason. We can put the pictures back side by side and leave that question where it belongs.

Next time you use an archive image, take one more look at the notes before you leave. What object gave you the picture on your screen?
