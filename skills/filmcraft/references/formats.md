# Choose a story form

| Form | Organizing question | Working unit | Test before picture lock |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Short factual film or product demo | What happened to someone, and what changed? | One decision, artifact and visible consequence | Can the viewer point to the moment the answer appears? |
| Explainer | What would a newcomer predict incorrectly? | Prediction → manipulation of one variable → observed result → transfer case | Give them a new case. Can they predict the result using the mechanism? |
| Documentary or profile | What can the available records and participants establish? | Scene, interview select, observational sound, archive | Can an editor trace each quote, date and inference back to its source? |
| Animated narrative | What does a character want, what do they try, and what is the cost? | Staged action with a readable anticipation, decision, reaction and new state | Watch silhouettes with dialogue muted. Does the choice still read? |
| Long tutorial or essay | Which smaller questions must be answered in order? | Self-contained chapters, each with a local promise and handoff | Can each chapter be summarized in a different sentence? Can the last exercise be completed? |

## Longer work

Budget runtime at chapter level, then reserve measured time for interviews, demonstrations, pauses and transitions. Example for 12:00: cold open 0:40; first account 2:20; competing explanation 2:30; test 3:00; consequences 2:30; return 1:00. These times sum to twelve minutes. Build individual chapters with their own pace. Cut the cold open if a patient observation is the stronger opening.

For each chapter keep its incoming question, new evidence, change of understanding, outgoing question and estimated/timed duration. Maintain a single index for recurring people, objects, maps, dates, credits and first explanations. At assembly, cut duplicate introductions. Allow a later discovery to invalidate an earlier voiceover. Include a transition that tells us why we move in place or time. Rebudget from actual tape before deciding on pickups.

## Animated characters

Write playable verbs. “She hides the torn corner under her thumb” gives the performer more to work with than “she feels anxious.” Lock character proportions, clothing, screen direction, hand occupancy, prop damage and eye line across shots. Establish the master pose; push in on the choice; hold the reaction until the audience can read it. For a synthetic character specify consistent reference art and voice, label reconstructions where a viewer might mistake them for recorded events.

## A second sample scene (fiction)

A child wants the last paper boat to float. First shot: at water level the mast slowly lists to the right. Hold the horizon still. The child lifts the heavy coin from the right deck, hesitates, then lays it across the centre crease. Cut wide only when both hands let go. Dialogue: “Your turn.” End wide enough to see who receives the boat. Sound: wet paper first, the other child’s shoes entering the puddle last. If the boat instantly rights itself before the coin moves, reset the boards; the cause has disappeared.
