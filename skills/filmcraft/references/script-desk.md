# The script desk

## Start from one thing the viewer can inspect

Fill these four lines with nouns specific to the commission:

- Someone wants ___; at this moment they expect ___.
- The camera or source can show ___, which complicates that expectation.
- To find out why, we follow ___ through ___.
- On the last shot, the viewer can now explain or predict ___.

A lyrical film can start with a sound or gesture and end with its changed meaning. A tutorial can start with a failed attempt and end with a reproducible test. Use a question the material actually answers.

## Three repairs for weak writing

| Test | Weak draft | Revision and why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Swap the subject. Does the line still fit a hundred films? | “Behind every booklet lies an incredible process.” | “On the printer’s sheet, page sixteen sits beside page one.” Two numbers and their position create a puzzle the camera can show. |
| Cover the pictures. Can a listener track who did what? | “Following imposition, correct sequencing is achieved upon assembly.” | “Fold the sheet. One becomes the front cover. Sixteen wraps around to the back.” Give the listener a hand, an action and a result. |
| Mute the voice. What changes on screen? | “Dynamic B-roll reveals the magic of printing.” | “Hold on the 16 at left. Keep the crease fixed as the left half folds away. Cut to the closed cover when 1 faces us.” The editor can draw both end frames and name the cut. |

A line that simply names what viewers already see earns its place if accessibility, unfamiliar vocabulary, or timing makes that repetition valuable. Otherwise use the voice to supply motive, relationship, a missing time scale or a contradiction. Put the measurement, map or receipt on screen where the viewer can inspect it.

## Run a table read

Read into a recorder in the intended register. Mark the first place you lose the subject, stumble over a number, or have to rush to finish. Rewrite that line where you stumbled. Put a spoken approximation in the VO and an exact sourced figure on screen if precision matters. Preserve an unbroken performance across cutaways. Save pickups by scene ID so the editor can replace one thought.

For planning only, use the creator’s measured words per minute. At 135 words/minute, 90 seconds containing 18 seconds of deliberate silence leaves about 162 spoken words. Interviews, comedy, translated lines and dramatic pauses have their own timing. Measure before promising length. A quiet shot may need longer than a sentence to read.

## Return in this order

1. **Clean script** — performer-ready lines grouped by character or scene. Put direction on a separate line outside the spoken block. Write numerals and acronym pronunciations the way the voice should say them.
2. **A/V edit** — one row per meaningful change. Columns: estimated or measured interval, exact spoken cue or sync sound, source/asset ID, picture at entry → picture at exit, on-screen text, and sound/cut.
3. **Pickup list** — missing quote, recording, footage, clearance or measurement; name the scene it affects and the fallback.
4. **Readback** — ask “What can you establish, and what remains open?” Ask what caused an event only if the sources establish a cause. Underline the exact words and point to the frame that communicate uncertainty. For a prototype, “The inventor hopes this lip will catch the drips. Let's see where they would land” can accompany a labeled design drawing. An animated dry floor presented as an observed outcome overstates the evidence.

Check montage as well as sentences. If tape says “I still keep it,” a cut to a stamp can assign a referent the interview never named. Hold on the interview or a neutral frame until the source identifies the object. An ambiguous date on a card establishes the inscription; verify its meaning before calling it a due date.

## Editor’s rehearsal

### Copyable pattern for untested designs

When the source is a sketch, put this structure in the recording block itself: “This drawing shows [documented feature]. The idea is to [intended outcome]. Would it work when [test condition]? We haven't tested that yet.” Choose natural equivalents in the film's language. For a wordless film the opening label states the design status, and the ending shows the proposed test. Keep that status legible on any visualization of an outcome.

For a pencil sketch of an indicator, don't invent what each color means. A proposed red/green mapping supplied by the filmmaker would still need a redundant word or shape. Read the draft a second time with the brief, commission, and all editor notes hidden. Rewrite any remaining declarative outcome a viewer could mistake for measured performance.

Give the same excerpt three readings: a listener with eyes shut, an editor with audio muted, and a skeptical fact checker. Record one concrete defect, the actual replacement line or shot, and the evidence behind the change. A numeric score is optional. Passing structural checks cannot establish originality, emotional truth or factual accuracy.
